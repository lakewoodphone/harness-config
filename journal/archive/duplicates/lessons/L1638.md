**L1638 · A merged fact must carry its own trust grade, or the loudest label wins**

MEASURED 2026-09-15 on the live index, after adding two real name sources.

WHAT WENT WRONG. The person layer had one rule for picking a display name out of the
candidate names on a cluster: prefer a two-word name, then the longest. `counterparty` --
a caller-ID string whose value can be anything, including a job description -- was folded
in with everything else and given no grade. So on the number with the most communications
in the database (1,669), the two-word label "Microsoft Word" beat every real name and
became the customer's name. An agent reading `person: "Microsoft Word"` on "Any chance u
can look at my laptop now?" concludes the message is from Microsoft.

The related measurement: `people.basis='name'` is NOT evidence of a person. It is evidence
that a label was person-SHAPED. Of 922 "named" people, ~63 matched a non-person pattern
(taking `att` inside "Mattis" as a false positive), and the shop's own case file for that
number names the customer "Pending customer".

THE RULE. Where a fact is assembled from sources of different quality, the QUALITY has to
be carried on the fact, not re-derived at every read. Anything that merges evidence --
union-find over identities, a display-name choice, a "best name" pick -- must keep a trust
grade per candidate and sort on it FIRST. Preference heuristics ("prefer two words") are
only safe WITHIN one trust tier. Retrofitting trust after the merge is not possible: by
then the wrong name is simply one of the candidates.

COROLLARY, and it is the more useful half: NEGATIVE evidence is evidence. The single best
fix here came from a record that says the customer's name is unknown -- "Pending
customer". That one fact is what let the index show the number instead of a job
description, on the busiest customer in the database. A source that says "I do not know"
is worth as much as a source that says a name, and it is the only thing that can ever
tell you a label is not a name.

Also learned: a name can arrive in several spellings of the same slug ("nashi nueman" vs
"Nashi Neuman") and a set-iteration order decided which one the owner would read; the
tiebreak now prefers a capitalised spelling.
