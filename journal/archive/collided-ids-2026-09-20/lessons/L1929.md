**L1929 · CORRECTION to L1926/L1928: a USB power-cycle does NOT isolate card from reader - use the throughput test, and never assume a tiny capacity is a fallback**

**CORRECTION (2026-09-17, issued 14:20) — L1926 and L1928 teach one thing that is WRONG: a USB port
power-cycle does NOT isolate the card from the reader. Corrected by a new entry rather than edited,
because a future session must be able to see that the seat got this wrong.**

**What I claimed and taught.** In `L1926` I wrote that the replayed block *"CHANGED across a USB port
power-cycle … = a live NAND page read badly, i.e. the CARD not the bridge"*, and in the SD playbook I
turned that into a general triage rule: an identical block within a session that *changes* across a
power-cycle proves the card rather than the bridge. **Both are non-sequiturs.**
`echo 0 > /sys/bus/usb/devices/<dev>/authorized` **re-enumerates the bridge itself** and does not
necessarily drop the card's 3.3 V, so a **bridge-side** volatile buffer changes across it just as
readily. The test discriminates nothing. The playbook row has been rewritten and the wrong row struck.

**What actually clears the reader** (use these three, they are stronger and free):
1. This reader, **this port and this LUN** (2-4, `12:0:0:1`), read a **58.6 GiB card** at 17.3–18.2 MB/s.
2. With the slot empty it reports **0 blocks**.
3. A read on the **empty LUN** returns **`Not Ready / Medium not present`**.
Plus it **parses and range-checks the LBA field** — READ(10) at LBA 8,388,608 returns
`Check Condition / Illegal Request / "Logical block address out of range"`.

**The better test that replaces the power-cycle claim — √ THE THROUGHPUT TEST.** Measure MB/s on the
patient and compare to a healthy card on the same reader/port/LUN. Ours: healthy **17.3–18.2 MB/s**,
patient **10.1 MB/s**. **A bridge replaying one internal sector for every LBA would be FASTER than a
real card, not ~3× slower than its own demonstrated rate.** That is the evidence the bytes are crossing
the SD interface, and it costs one `dd`.

**Three more clauses of the Goldstein write-up that did not survive the same adversarial pass, recorded
so they are not repeated:**
- **"Fallen back on a minimal default capacity" — STRUCK.** 121.25 MiB is a clean SDSC CSD decode
 (485 × 256 KiB) and is exactly what a genuine **128 MB-class** card reports; documented fallback
 capacities are 512 KB, 2 GB, 30/31/32 MB, 46 MB. The honest claim is "the card presents 121.25 MiB
 and returns one identical block at every address; whether that capacity is genuine or a fallback is
 **not established**". **A tiny reported capacity is not automatically a fault — read the label.**
- **"The NAND is intact" — STRUCK as UNTESTED.** Nothing observes the NAND, and the lab route needs it
 intact *and* un-scrambled (the lab in one case had to reverse the controller's scrambling). Any lab
 quote must therefore be **success-only**.
- **"Unaddressable from any host" — WEAKENED to "from this host and this reader".** One host, one
 reader, one OS is not "any host".

**And the one trap that must never be sprung:** the only documented escape from a tiny-capacity state is
a **camera format** (SanDisk forum: a 32 GB card reporting 512 KB came back as 31.2 GB, data destroyed).
**Decline every format prompt on every host** — and *that* rule now outlives the wrong one it replaced.

**Two supporting claims I got wrong and corrected in the case file:** `serial 000000001539` is
**`bcdDevice 15.39` zero-padded** — a model-wide string, not a unit identifier; and the "16 GB / 29 GB
cards" this reader supposedly read were **Newman's USB flash drives** on other ports, not SD cards.
The lesson underneath: **check what a control actually control-led for before citing it.**
