**L1978 · Check the thing, not its status field: a dead camera reports state=idle, and 8 of 9 real faults were a rename**

A security check that alarms on the STATE field cannot see a dead camera, and a check that
alarms on every unavailable entity cannot be believed. Both failure modes appeared in the
same hour, on the same system, from the same field.

1. ALARMING ON TOO MUCH. The new HA absence check reported "nine real faults - the
 waiting-room camera tree is down, the apartment door lock is down". 8 of the 9 carried
 HA's `restored: true` attribute, which means the integration no longer provides the
 entity - a leftover from a rename - and every one had a WORKING TWIN under a renamed id
 (`cam_waiting_room_alarm_local` vs `..._local_2`, `cam_waiting_room_sub` vs
 `..._sub_4`). Nothing was blind. Unchecked, the check would have fired 8 false alarms on
 every run forever AND told the owner his cameras were down. Fix: `restored` is its own
 class, a leftover, never a fault. Exit code no longer moves for it.

2. ALARMING ON TOO LITTLE, WHICH IS WORSE. The genuinely serious finding was invisible to
 the state field entirely: six cameras in Larry's area report `state=idle` - a healthy
 reading - and cannot produce a snapshot. The state says fine; the image is gone. A check
 that reads only `state` reports them healthy forever. Only fetching the image finds it,
 and only the DIRECT read on the host that can reach HA does that: the same check reported
 1 fault over ssh and 7 on the host, and the difference was exactly the snapshot failures.

RULES
(a) For anything whose PURPOSE is producing a thing, check the thing, not its status. A
 camera's job is an image; a printer's job is a page; a cron job's job is an effect. The
 status field is what the device says about itself.
(b) When a count goes up, ask which field produced it and whether a second field contradicts
 it. `restored`, `before_exists`, `ever_replied`, `applied` - each of these decided whether
 a number meant "the world is broken" or "our bookkeeping is".
(c) Before reporting N faults, list the N and look at whether they share a shape: 8 of 9
 sharing an identical `last_changed` microsecond cluster and a `_2`-suffixed twin was the
 tell. A cluster is a rename, not an outage.
(d) A check whose two code paths disagree is telling you one of them is not measuring the
 same thing. Here: 1 fault over ssh vs 7 on the host. Investigate the DIFFERENCE rather
 than picking the number you prefer - the stricter path was the correct one.
