**L3901 · Three ways an unattended copy job lies: findmnt path args, rsync parents, unsorted manifest compare**

## Three ways an unattended job lies to you, measured in one night on WO26101812753

The rig that wrote the customer's 64 GB stick failed three times before it passed. Every failure was
mine and each one generalises.

**1. A tool's path argument is not always a path.** `findmnt -no TARGET "$PATH"` treats its argument as
a **device or target spec**, not a filename. It therefore reported a directory on an already-mounted
drive as "not mounted", and the job then tried to mount that drive — which another session had mounted
and was writing to. The check that works is about the thing you actually care about:
`[ -d "$STAGE/Users/semah" ]`. Read the tool's argument contract, do not assume the name of the flag.

**2. rsync creates only the FINAL path component.** Copying to `/mnt/stick/Users/semah/` on a freshly
formatted volume dies with `mkdir ".../Users/semah" failed: No such file or directory` and **rc=11**
because `Users/` does not exist and rsync will not create it. This is correct rsync behaviour and
catches everybody once: create the intermediate directories yourself.

**3. Comparing two enumerations without sorting compares order, not content.** `find` walks two
different exFAT volumes in different orders, so a sha256 comparison of the two manifests reported 36
**identical** files as "missing here, extra there" and produced a **false VERIFY FAIL** — after 51 GB
had already been written correctly. A false FAIL is not the safe direction: it nearly cost a second
full write, and the instinct it creates ("the copy is bad, try again") is exactly what you must not do
to a good copy.

## The rule

**A verification that has not been shown to fail for the right reason is not a verification.** Sort,
normalise, then compare; and when a comparison fails, read the diff and check whether the failure is
about content at all before touching the data. The three signals that settled this one: equal line
counts on both sides, the same paths appearing in both the removed and added sets, and identical
hashes printed for those paths — all three together mean ordering, never corruption.

Second rule, from the same night: **an unattended job needs a stop that cannot be argued with.** This
rig refuses to touch any device that does not match an armed serial *and* capacity *and* model; it
refused the customer's SSD and the bench SSD by serial; it refused a foreign filesystem rather than
wiping it; and it refused to write when only 2.4 GB was free against a 58 GB requirement. Four refusals
in one night, none of which needed a human to be present — which is the only reason it was safe to
leave running.
