**L5344 · A touch fault after a screen swap: test it in UEFI before you touch Windows - two minutes, no screws, and it decides software vs hardware**

**The cheapest test in the whole Surface job is the one no procedure mentions: touch in UEFI — and it must run BEFORE the software steps, not after.**

Bench, 2026-10-15, Greenfeld Surface Laptop 4 (1950) after a panel swap: image perfect, **touch dead**,
Device Manager reporting a device power failure. The written order at that point was: finish Windows and
firmware updates → reboot → Surface Diagnostic Toolkit → Touch Display (TDM) calibration → re-seat four
display connectors → put the old panel back. That order starts with an hour of software on a machine that
may have a hardware fault.

Microsoft's own answer to "touch stopped working after screen replacement" is a **two-minute test that
needs no tools and no disassembly**: shut down fully, hold **volume-up**, press power, keep holding
volume-up **past the logo**, and then **tap a menu item in Surface UEFI**. The keyboard works in UEFI, so
the screen stays navigable either way. Microsoft's wording: *"If touch does not work in UEFI, your device
likely has experienced a hardware failure."*
([Microsoft Q&A 2290897](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/2290897/how-do-i-fix-touch-screen-after-screen-replacement), read 2026-10-15)

**The reading, and why it is worth two minutes:**
- **Touch responds in UEFI** → the digitizer, its flex and the connector are alive, the new panel is
 exonerated for touch, and the fault is in Windows (driver, firmware, or the missing TDM calibration).
 The software steps are then the right steps.
- **Touch dead in UEFI** → hardware: connector or panel. Skip the software entirely, re-seat the
 connectors, and get to the old-panel control.

**The general rule:** when a symptom could be software or hardware, look for the vendor's own environment
that runs *without* the OS — firmware setup, a boot menu, a recovery shell — and test there first. It is
the only fork in the fault tree that costs no parts, no screws and no reinstalling of anything, and it is
usually absent from third-party teardown guides because they start at the screwdriver.
