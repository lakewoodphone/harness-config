**L3628 · 'Solvable by machines but not by humans' is incoherent for the formal results, and lands squarely on the practical ones**

**L (2026-10-12, secratary-cloud)** - The owner's challenge to his uncle's book turned on a claim worth keeping straight, because it will come up again: that the things Yanofsky called unsolvable ever ARE solvable, just not by humans.

**Why it fails for the formal results.** A human being is not a computational class of its own. Anything a person can do with pen, paper and patience is in principle reproducible by a machine, which is the physical Church-Turing thesis and also Yanofsky's own mechanist position. So if a machine could decide the halting problem, then a human with that machine could too, and the theorem would be false. There is no gap for a human-only solution to hide in. "Unprovable in S" is a property of a formal system, not of a species, and a machine is a formal system: no amount of machine intelligence lifts it. The escape hatch runs the other way from the one people reach for.

**Why it succeeds on the practical claims.** The book's rhetorical force comes from the practical kind of impossibility - "trillions of centuries", "faster computers will not help us". There, machines really are eating the claim: industrial SAT/SMT solvers, AlphaProof Nexus (9 of 353 formalized Erdos problems, 2 open 56 years, plus 44 OEIS conjectures), the 2026 OpenAI release claiming progress on 372 problems. His remedy is to narrow that claim, not abandon it; NP-hardness and worst-case hardness are not repealed.

**The distinction that decides it - open is not unsolvable.** Every problem AI has recently solved was OPEN, not proven unsolvable. Yanofsky never claimed open problems are unsolvable; he claimed some CLASSES are undecidable, and he made hard-versus-impossible his flagship point (the chessboard with two corners removed). Running the two together is the most common way this argument goes wrong, including by people making it in his favour.

**And the second half of the challenge, which is the better one:** "he does not understand math" fails on the record - twelve adversarial clusters found no mathematical error, only correct theorems carried further than they license (the halting problem applied to instances rather than to uniform procedures, "all is relative" attributed to relativity, a fallacious most-truths argument, a false cell-replacement figure). "Humans can only understand mathematics up to a point" HOLDS, and is now measurable: 42% of one release formalized at posting, ~50% released unconfirmed, three papers withdrawn inside a day for a sign error, and Terence Tao warning that AI writes proofs nobody can read. That is a limit on humans, not on truth - and it applies to whoever is making the objection, so it can only be used against a specific argument, never against a man.
