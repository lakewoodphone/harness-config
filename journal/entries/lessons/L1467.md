**L1467 · Grouping comms by the name in them merges strangers: measure the label vocabulary first**

## Grouping communications by the name in them merges strangers — measure the vocabulary first

The obvious way to build "one person, all their comms" is to group by the name on each
message. Measured on the real corpus 2026-09-14, that would have been badly wrong: the
names that appear across the most phone numbers are **carrier caller-ID locations**, not
people.

| name | distinct phone numbers |
|---|---|
| Pt Plsnt Bch Nj | 37 |
| Keyport NJ | 23 |
| Atlntic Hlds Nj | 20 |
| New Brunswck Nj | 20 |
| South River Nj | 17 |
| Merck Co Inc | 15 |

275 names appeared on more than one number, worst case 37. A naive grouping would have
fused 37 unconnected customers into one record, and — worse — an agent asking "what is the
story with this customer" would have received a stranger's conversations as though they
were theirs.

The rule that holds: a name may merge phones only when it (a) looks like a person — no
digits, no US state/territory token at the end, ≤4 words, not a known carrier string — and
(b) sits on **at most three** numbers. Everything else still names a record but never
merges two. Result: 1,225 people, 921 named, **zero carrier labels**, 2,199 aliases, and
49% of all 166,902 communications attributed to a person (the rest are one-off callers who
never gave a name — the honest ceiling).

Two rules that generalise:
* **Before grouping anything by a string field, count the distinct keys per value.** The
 distribution of "how many entities share this label" tells you whether the field is an
 identity or a label. It takes one query and it is the difference between a merge and a
 smear.
* Every alias records **why** it exists (`observed_name`, `address_book:contacts`, …) and
 which source produced it, so a wrong merge can be found and argued with later.

### The address book was the second trap: "known contact" is not "customer"

`contacts` carries its own `source`, and 90 of its 623 rows are `email_auto_discovered` —
addresses added mechanically because mail arrived from them. One of them is
`uber@uber.com`. Trusting "this address is on file" as evidence of a customer put Stripe
payouts and Lowe's marketing into a list of people waiting for an answer. The contact's
`source` now travels with the alias, so a consumer can ask for *manual* contacts and get
humans.
