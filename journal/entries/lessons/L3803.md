**L3803 · A subagent's 'correction' of a prior record is itself a claim: verify it against the primary source first - a wrong correction overwrites a right number and raises the cost of recovering it**

**L — A subagent's "correction" of a prior record is itself a claim, and must be verified against the primary source before it is believed.**

**RECORDED 2026-10-14**, during the portable-SSD stream of the handoff-media research (five mesh subagents, one re-check pass by the delegating session).

**What happened.** The shop's 2026-10-13 record stated the Kingston XS1000 has a **100 GB SLC cache**. The stream
assigned to verify it wrote, in its own deliverable, **"OVERTURNED / NOT SUPPORTED"**, asserting the cited source
"publishes no cache size at all for the XS1000" and that the ~100 GB figure "belongs to the XS2000". It carried
that verdict into its table, its summary, and its HOLES list.

**It was false.** everythingusb's table **does** publish an SLC-cache-size row, and that row reads **XS1000 = 100 GB,
XS2000 = 96 GB**. Re-read directly by the delegating session on 2026-10-14. The shop's record was right and the
"correction" was the error.

**Why this shape is dangerous, and why it is worse than an ordinary mistake.** A correction carries borrowed
authority: it arrives framed as diligence, it *supersedes* a prior record, and a later reader has less reason to
re-check it than to check a fresh claim — the work of checking appears to have been done. So a wrong correction
does not merely add a wrong number; it **overwrites a right one and raises the cost of recovering it.** Had the
re-check not happened, the shop's next session would have inherited a "corrected" record that was wrong in the
direction of believing a source said less than it did.

**The mechanical cause, which generalises.** The stream read the article's **prose** — where the cache size is
indeed never stated in words — and concluded the source had no cache figure. The number was in the article's
**comparison table**, one row down from the numbers it had successfully extracted. Extraction that reads prose
and skips tables will systematically under-report what a source says, and "the source doesn't say it" is the
exact conclusion such a failure produces. Compare: the same stream quoted that article's post-cache figures
correctly, because those *are* discussed in prose.

**The rule.** (1) When a source is a table, enumerate the table's **rows**, not just the sentences around it —
`the source does not say X` is only sayable after you have listed what the source's structure has slots for.
(2) A claim that **overturns** an existing record is a higher-consequence claim than one that fills a gap, and
gets a correspondingly harder check — a reversal should cite the specific row or line, not the absence of one.
(3) In a verification pass, **re-check the corrections first.** They are the claims a reader is least likely to
re-check, so they are where a residual error does the most damage.

**Second instance, same session, different cause.** A second stream reported the Samsung T7 Shield 2 TB as
"993 MB/s average, no cache cliff observed" over a 300 GB write. The quotation was accurate — the tester does say
the test "did not exhaust the cache" — but **the tester's own spec box for that exact drive states pSLC cache
">300 GB approx."**, which is *why* a 300 GB write did not exhaust it, and which **disqualifies the number as a
post-cache floor.** The stream had logged the cache size as an unresolved conflict against another reviewer's
~48 GB estimate; the spec box on the same page resolved it. **The lesson there: when a measurement reports "no
cliff", the first thing to check is whether the test was long enough to reach one — and the cache size is often
printed on the same page as the result that failed to find it.**
