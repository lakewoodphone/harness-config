**L1935 · Two failed applies in one round - match the corpus serialisation, and verify a matcher's selectivity before it writes**

# L1928 — two failed applies in one round: the ensure_ascii rewrite and the 6.5x over-fire

## What I tried, and what went wrong twice

**Attempt 1 — 226 files rewritten for 28 intended changes.** Writing `sourcePath` corrections into 383
sync records, I reserialised each record with `json.dumps(rec, indent=2, ensure_ascii=False)`. The corpus
stores non-ASCII as ESCAPES (`\u2014`); my serialisation emitted the literal characters instead. So every
record whose prose contained an em-dash showed both a `-` and a `+` line for the whole long
`internalSummary`, `portalSummary` and `nextAction`. **28 corrections became a 226-file diff with 1,532
churn lines.** The VALUES were right and the diff was unreadable — which for a customer-record change is
the same as wrong, because nobody can review it.

**Attempt 2 — 183 corrections where 28 were safe.** The similarity test compared the record's stem
against case-file stems and counted a shared **date prefix** (`2026-08-24`) as a matching token. Records
whose descriptions only partly overlapped therefore scored as TOTAL matches and qualified for a rewrite.

**Both runs reverted; nothing committed.** The second was walked back only after applying to the
worktree, which is why the revert mattered.

## The rules

1. **Match the corpus's own serialisation, not your language's default.** Before rewriting structured
 files, diff your serialiser's output against ONE unchanged file. That single check would have caught
 attempt 1 in seconds — and it is cheap because it changes nothing.
2. **A guard that verifies your own change is worth more than the change.** After attempt 1 I added a
 check that refuses unless EXACTLY the intended line differs. It did not catch attempt 2 (the
 over-fire passed it — one line each), but it converts "did I reformat?" from a hope into a rule.
3. **Verify the matcher's SELECTIVITY before letting it write, not after.** 183 vs 28 was knowable from
 the dry run: the number was 6.5× the analysis. A proposal count that far above the measured set IS
 the bug report. I read that number and applied anyway.
4. **A date is not a name.** Any token-set similarity over files named `<date>-<description>` versus
 `<description>-<date>` will over-match on the date alone. Strip known-value tokens (dates, not just
 stopwords) from both sides before comparing — a token that appears in EVERY filename carries no
 information about which file is meant.
5. **Registering a fix as report-only is a legitimate outcome.** F04's handler works and validates its
 target; its matcher is wrong. `writes: False` plus a `blocked_reason` states exactly that, so the
 diagnosis ships and the incorrect action cannot run. Partial delivery with an honest boundary beats
 either withholding the finding or shipping the wrong write.

## Why I stopped instead of trying a third time

Two failed attempts and a revert had already consumed the round. A third speculative apply across 183
customer records, late in a long session, is the trade this session has twice proved wrong: round 9's
overwritten production sync tool and rounds 15–16's wasted conflicted rebase both came from pushing on
after something did not work. **The measurement is durable; the next attempt starts from it.**

## Evidence

- Attempt 1: `git diff --shortstat` → `226 files changed, 1532 insertions(+), 1507 deletions(-)`.
- Attempt 2: `183 corrections` against a measured near-certain set of **28**; `43 refused by the guard`.
- Both reverted: `git diff --name-only docs/customer-operations/sync-records/` → empty.
- The real defect, measured and kept: 161 records self-referential, 39 `cases/`-relative, 41 other.
