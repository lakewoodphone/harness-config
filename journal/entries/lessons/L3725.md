**L3725 · Never act on a blink-code reading the counter is unsure of: film it and count one complete cycle**

**Seen on the Inspiron 15 3520 bench reading, 2026-10-12 (WO26100812737).** The same machine was read twice, minutes apart, by the same person: *"two orange, then four white"* and *"first the first five seconds i think it was doing 3 orange 5 white but i'm not totally sure"*. On Dell's table for that model those are not neighbouring codes with similar meaning - **2/4 is Memory/RAM failure** (a part) and **3/5 is Power rail failure, resolution "replace the system board"** (a dead machine). The whole difference between a 90-dollar job and a write-off was a possible miscount.

**Why a partial count invents a code.** These LEDs cycle their pattern continuously with a pause between groups, so a reading that starts mid-cycle presents the tail of one group followed by the head of the next. Depending on where you start watching, the same machine honestly looks like 2 amber/4 white, or 4 amber, or 3 amber/5 white - and pairs a partial count produces need not appear in the vendor's table at all.

**The rule: never act on a blink-code reading the counter is unsure of.** Film about 20 seconds and count one complete group from its first amber blink. A phone video is a permanent, countable record of something that lasts two seconds and cannot be replayed otherwise, and it costs ten seconds. On a machine whose diagnostic LED is the only output it still has, the count **is** the diagnosis - an unsure count is not a reading, and the cost of the wrong code is the customer's machine.

**Corollary for the bench sequence:** when two codes are possible, choose the next test that discriminates between them rather than the next test on the plan. Here that is a known-good module in each slot (module versus slot versus board) plus one filmed count - not a second reseat, which cannot move the answer.
