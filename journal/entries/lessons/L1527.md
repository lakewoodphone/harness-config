**L1527 · Gating destructive work on verification: fail closed, never compare against an input that does not exist yet, and do not run three 13 GB scans at once**

Three rules for verification passes and for gating anything destructive on them, all measured on the
Newman flash-drive job 2026-09-15. Two of my own passes were wrong before the third was right; the
gate caught both.

**1. A gate must fail closed, and it must be checked by a machine, not by me reading a log.**
The delivery script refused to format anything unless `notes/VERIFIED-OK` existed *and* the live
file-count/size still matched the numbers inside it, *and* `clamscan` was clean, *and* `lsblk -dno
SERIAL` matched each expected drive serial, re-read immediately before `mkfs`. Two of my three
verification passes were buggy; both times the script printed "ABORTED — NOTHING FORMATTED, NOTHING
WRITTEN" and stopped. Design rule: never let a destructive step depend on my having eyeballed output —
give it a marker file with the numbers in it, and re-read the hardware identity at the moment of use.

**2. Never start a comparison before both of its inputs exist.**
My v3 pass waited for the *source*-side manifest only, then compared it against a copy-side manifest
that verify2 had not written yet. An empty file read as "454 user files not copied" — a false alarm on
the most alarming possible finding. `wc -l` both inputs and refuse to judge if either is empty; better
still, wait on the specific artifact you need, never on "the other job".

**3. Redundant verification passes multiply the expensive part, and the expensive part is usually the
slowest device in the building.**
Three passes × (hash 11.16 GB off a USB 2.0 flash drive + `clamscan -r` over 12.97 GB, twice each in
one case) put four processes on the same data and turned a ~40-minute job into hours. Throughput on
that bench while three readers shared one xHCI controller: ~13-15 MB/s total. Cost is not just time:
every extra scan is another chance for a *confidently wrong* answer to be the one that gets read.
Decide which single pass is authoritative, run that one, and kill its precedessors — but by explicit
PID, because `pkill -f` in an ssh one-liner matches the ssh command line itself (see L1514).

Related, and worth remembering for the next "the customer says it has a virus" intake: `fatattr` on
the read-only mount is what distinguishes "the worm hid their files" from "the worm only added
decoys". Here it was only decoys — the customer's files carried ordinary attributes — and the honest
answer to the customer is different in each case.
