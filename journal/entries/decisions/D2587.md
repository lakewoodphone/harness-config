**D2587 · 2026-09-24 · Shlock drive, final for tonight: the loose rope in the pocket is dead (slips, and the load lands on an unreinforced mouth); put the leading pole BACK IN and drive its ends; the real water fix is lowering the hanger stack, not removing the pole.**

**Decision, 2026-09-24 evening (Thursday, the night before Sukkos), after a second seven-angle fleet on the drive and the no-front-pole question.**

**1. The loose rope through the empty leading pocket is DEAD.** Two independent agents reached it separately: a slack rope in a straight fabric pocket is a slip-fit liner, not a pull member - whole-length friction is 0.3-0.9 lb against the 17.5 lb per mouth needed, so it slides through; all the load then lands on stopper knots bearing on an **unreinforced pocket mouth with no grommet, no webbing and no patch**, i.e. strictly weaker than the tarp's weakest rated point. Knots of that size are also smaller than the mouth slot and can walk out. Do not thread a loose rope and call it a drive.

**2. The leading pole goes BACK IN, and the drive is built on its two ends.** That is tonight's job and it is the tested path: feed the pole, fence each protruding end with a hose clamp (or cross-bolt it with the rope on a shackle), one line for IN from the two ends over a wheel on each house-end post with the bight in the operator's hand, one line for OUT over a wheel on each yard-end post, both cleated at one station, and the 300 lb scale in line for the first pull (~40 lb dry expected). Rationale: of everything tried, the pole is the only member that is rigid, positive, already on site, and the design of record; a sewn 2-in webbing member stitched to the band at the 8 hanger stations is better in principle (stitching replaces friction) but is a sewing job that cannot be finished tonight.

**3. Removing the pole buys very little on water, and the record says it was never the dam.** The free hem sags 0.45-0.80 in (best ~0.5 in) between hangers, which shrinks the pool from about 50 lb to roughly 7-25 lb - an improvement, not a drain. The thing that actually holds the water is the **hanger stack, 1.29 in above the rail plane**, which is 8.7x what the pole removal buys. And the corpus's own state of play says the poles were never in the pockets when the 0.79-in pool was measured - so the measured failure state is the pole-out state.

**4. The water fix, on a dry day, is therefore ONE piece of work: lower the tarp's front attachment.** Turn the 8 hanger sockets from 12 o'clock to 6 o'clock (a 4.07-in swing, no parts, two people, ~30 min), or the equivalent rope sling under each sleeve (3.2 in). Guard the yard beam with a UHMW offcut; re-set the retract stop by hand. Nothing else on the cover changes, and nothing rides the rails.

**5. Fit thin UHMW liners in the 8 sleeves before the season turns.** With no stiff front edge the sleeves have no equaliser, their 0.170-in play is marginal (3.2 deg per-side / 6.5 deg strict against a 5.7-8.5 deg UHMW-on-steel friction angle), and a cocked sleeve that jams sends the pull into the fabric and tears a grommet. A 1-3/4 in ID x 2 in OD liner cut to 3 in drops the play to 0.045 in (0.9 deg). Stock item, no machining.

**6. Cheap guards, both no-cost:** tie the two 24-in corner overhangs down with rope (the rails stop 24 in short of each side, so those corners are the first thing to tear); and close the two leading-edge pocket mouths with cable ties so the empty pocket cannot scoop water during the stroke.

**7. The storm decision stands and is separate from tonight's job:** a nor'easter with 35-50 mph gusts is forecast Friday to Sunday. At 50 mph the uplift is 2,627 lb = 164 lb per point against a #4 grommet's 150-200 lb tear-out, and the cover's own failure point is about 43 mph; even furled the bundle takes 446 lb over 8 hangers. The cover comes off the frame before Friday sunset - it is taken down, not stowed on the rails.
