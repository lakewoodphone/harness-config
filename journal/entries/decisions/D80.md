**D80 · 2026-09-14 · The owner's answer on the product's promise: no numbers until measured, and make the words carry it (his words: option 1, "but use 3 to make it sound good").**

Asked as one question with three options (`QUESTIONS.md`, held behind the legal question): publish nothing numeric until we have measured it ourselves, publish a measured figure after the labelling pilot, or never publish a number and rely on the qualitative promise. He chose **the first**, with the third's language carrying the product instead of an apology.
*Implemented:* both customer-facing documents now lead with what the filter **does** promise — *"when the filter is not sure, it hides. Every time, on the safe side, without asking."* — then name plainly what it will not pretend (that it sees everything), and say about numbers that we quote none yet, and that when we do they will arrive with the measurements behind them, *"because a number without those is not a measurement, it is advertising"*. The notice and the terms carry the same sentence, which the notice already claimed they did.
*Made enforceable:* `server/tests/test_public_claims.py` (5 tests) reads both documents and fails if a digit-percentage appears, if the deny-first sentence is gone, or if the two disagree. A policy about a sentence needs a test about a sentence — and this is the exact shape the rule fails in: someone adds a reassuring figure to a page, and the harmless-looking sentence becomes the exposure. The FTC's case against NGL Labs turned on an overstated AI-filtering claim standing alone, with no privacy law involved.
