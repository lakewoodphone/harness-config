## HANDOFF — the person concept is in, and now it actually filters

Round 3. `main` was `8d52a34`; **PR #27** carries the person concept. The finding of the round is not
that the concept was built but that **it did not filter anything until the last hour**.

## THE FINDING: a concept that never reached a decision

The delegated workstream delivered exactly what its brief asked: `persons`, `person_rules` and
`device_persons` tables with additive migrations for SQLite and Postgres; a person rules layer shaped
like the existing four; nine owner routes under `/owner/api/people`, all registered in
`route_security.py`; a documented cascade position; and 17 passing tests including "two people on one
phone resolve different effective rules".

**None of it affected a single filtering decision.** The composer every SOCKS decision goes through,
`socks_runtime_state.effective_rules`, did `base -> tenant -> household -> phone` and stopped there.
The person-aware composer, `rule_access_ops.compose_effective_rules_for_phone_context`, is called by
the owner dashboard, the AI-profile workflow and the phones router — three paths that **show** rules
and decide nothing.

So a parent could create a person, assign a device, set that person's rules, watch the dashboard
render them, and the device would keep filtering by the household's rules alone.

**Why the 17 tests did not catch it, which is the transferable part:** they asserted through the
dashboard's composer. Every one of them was correct about the thing it tested and blind to the seam
that mattered. A green suite over the wrong entry point is the shape of this whole round.

**Fixed** in `21e3184`: the person layer now composes in `socks_runtime_state.effective_rules`, after
the phone's overrides and before the community floor, through the restrictive merge so a person can
tighten a device but never loosen what the household or community require. The invalidation contract
was already honoured — `routers/owner_people.py` bumps the rules version on every write that changes
a person's rules or an assignment, and the composition cache is keyed on it.

**Proven by deliberate break, twice:** removing the person step from `socks_runtime_state` fails
exactly one test — the new enforcement case, whose failure message is *"the SOCKS decision path does
not enforce per-person settings"* — and removing it from `rule_access_ops` fails exactly the three
composition tests there. Both restored byte-identical. The new test is deliberately aimed at the
broken entry point and carries its own control (before any assignment the domain must not already be
denied).
